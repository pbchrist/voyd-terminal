# Storytelling Judgment Rubric

## Purpose

This rubric is designed to answer three separate questions that are often blurred together:

1. **Does the story function?** — causality, clarity, continuity, and payoff.
2. **Is the story dramatically effective?** — tension, character pressure, escalation, increasing stakes, surprise, and emotional consequence.
3. **Is the story artistically distinctive?** — voice, thematic depth, imagery, invention, and the feeling that this particular story had to exist.

For interactive fiction, a fourth question applies:

4. **Does interactivity create meaning that a linear version could not?**

Score each category from **0–5**, but do not let the total score replace judgment. A catastrophic failure in a load-bearing category can sink a story regardless of its average.

**Do not produce a single aggregate quality score such as 82/100.** Category scores are diagnostic coordinates, not a substitute for artistic judgment.

Before applying the rubric, identify the work’s form, genre, scale, intended audience experience, and governing dramatic mode. Mark categories genuinely inapplicable rather than forcing every story into the same architecture.

## Scoring Scale

| Score | Meaning |
|---:|---|
| 0 | Missing, incoherent, or actively damaging |
| 1 | Present only nominally; major failure |
| 2 | Partly functional but weak, generic, confusing, or inconsistent |
| 3 | Solid and effective; professional baseline |
| 4 | Strong, memorable, and well integrated |
| 5 | Exceptional; the element generates meaning throughout the story |

## Part I: Load-Bearing Story Architecture

These categories determine whether the story stands up at all.

### 1. Dramatic proposition

**Question:** What specific dramatic situation makes this story worth entering?

A strong proposition contains a character, a pressure, an unresolved conflict, and an implied transformation. It should generate scenes rather than merely describe a setting.

**Look for:**

- A situation that produces difficult action
- An immediate source of instability
- A contradiction with room to deepen
- A reason this story begins now

**Failure signs:** atmospheric premise without drama; lore replacing conflict; a protagonist who could walk away without cost.

### 2. Character objective and pressure

**Question:** What does the central character want now, and what forces that desire to evolve?

The objective need not be stated, but it must organize behavior. Pressure should reveal competing desires, values, fears, loyalties, or identities.

**Look for:** active pursuit; meaningful resistance; changing tactics; choices that expose character.

**Failure signs:** passive observation; arbitrary action; characters serving plot instructions instead of personal imperatives.

### 3. Causality

**Question:** Does each major beat happen because of what came before?

Coincidence may begin trouble, but decisions should propagate it. The reader should feel both surprise and retrospective inevitability.

**Look for:** choice → consequence → altered situation → new choice.

**Failure signs:** episodic events; revelations without preparation; characters forgetting obvious solutions; outcomes determined by authorial convenience.

### Supplemental diagnostic: Causal ownership

**Question:** Who causes the decisive change?

A protagonist can appear active while the important turns are actually caused by coincidence, antagonist mistakes, outside rescue, revelations delivered without pursuit, system fiat, or authorial intervention. Strong storytelling gives meaningful causal ownership to the characters whose choices matter.

**Look for:** the protagonist causes, provokes, discovers, or meaningfully contributes to decisive turns; important knowledge is earned through action; the antagonist's defeat emerges from pressure rather than convenient stupidity; in interactive fiction, the player's intention contributes causally to later state.

**Failure signs:** protagonist mainly witnesses decisive events; crucial information simply arrives; antagonist conveniently self-destructs; choices alter presentation but not causal history; player actions do not materially contribute to later conditions.

### 4. Escalation and increasing stakes

**Question:** Does pressure increase by changing the nature of the problem, and do the consequences of failure become more significant?

Strong escalation does more than increase volume. It narrows options, raises cost, complicates knowledge, deepens personal stakes, and forces increasingly revealing action.

**Stakes escalation:** as the story progresses, the consequences of success, failure, delay, or refusal should become more significant, more personal, and harder to reverse.

**Diagnostic question:** If the protagonist fails later in the story, is the loss meaningfully greater, more intimate, or more irreversible than it was earlier?

**Look for:** irreversible consequences; shrinking safe territory; deeper personal stakes; greater cost of delay or refusal; transformed conflict; loss of previously available options; external stakes becoming personally specific.

**Failure signs:** repeated arguments; interchangeable obstacles; danger increasing without meaning increasing; multiple scenes doing the same dramatic job; harder obstacles with unchanged consequences.

### 5. Turning points

**Question:** Does new information or action genuinely reorganize the story?

A turning point changes what the character wants, believes, risks, or can do. It is not simply an exciting event.

**Look for:** altered strategy; reinterpreted past; new cost; loss of an old option.

**Failure signs:** revelations followed by business as usual; twists that create surprise without consequence.

### Supplemental diagnostic: Irreversibility

**Question:** What becomes impossible, lost, exposed, committed, damaged, known, or transformed because this unit occurred?

Important dramatic units should leave residue.

**Look for:** knowledge that cannot be unknown; trust that cannot be fully restored; commitment that closes another option; exposure of a secret; new debt or obligation; permanent shift in status; altered relationship; changed physical reality; moral compromise; sacrifice.

**Failure signs:** a scene can be removed without changing later behavior; confrontation leaves no relational consequence; revelation is forgotten; branches reconverge by erasing their differences; dramatic action produces temporary excitement but no persistent state.

### 6. Climax and resolution

**Question:** Does the climax force the story’s deepest conflict into decisive action?

The climax should require knowledge, cost, and character development accumulated by the preceding story. The resolution should show what the decision changed.

**Look for:** an earned final choice; convergence of external and internal conflict; consequence rather than explanation.

**Failure signs:** arbitrary rescue; villain collapse; final choice unrelated to earlier dilemmas; ending immediately after the plot mechanism stops.

## Part II: Character and Relationship Drama

### 7. Character specificity

**Question:** Could this character be replaced by a familiar archetype without changing the story?

**Look for:** distinctive perception; personal logic; contradiction; behavior shaped by history rather than biography dumps.

**Failure signs:** competence as personality; trauma as personality; dialogue voices differing only by vocabulary.

### 8. Internal contradiction

**Question:** What does the character simultaneously want and resist?

Contradiction creates dramatic depth when both sides are credible and active.

**Look for:** values in conflict; self-protective habits that obstruct conscious goals; decisions that cost part of the self.

**Failure signs:** a flaw merely announced; easy self-awareness; transformation without resistance.

### 9. Relationship dynamics

**Question:** Do relationships change through leverage, intimacy, knowledge, debt, betrayal, dependence, or power?

**Look for:** shifting advantage; subtext; asymmetric knowledge; actions that redefine the relationship.

**Failure signs:** static banter; characters exchanging exposition; conflict without changed relational stakes.

### 10. Antagonistic intelligence

**Question:** Does the opposing force adapt intelligently to the protagonist?

The antagonist may be a person, institution, environment, metaphysical law, or the protagonist’s own pattern.

**Look for:** counterstrategy; credible advantage; pressure tailored to the protagonist’s weakness.

**Failure signs:** antagonist waits to be defeated; opposition acts irrationally to preserve the plot; power without perceptiveness.

### 11. Transformation

**Question:** Is change demonstrated through altered action under comparable pressure?

**Look for:** the character faces a later version of an earlier dilemma and acts differently; transformation carries loss or cost.

**Failure signs:** speeches declaring growth; personality replacement; change produced solely by information.

## Part III: Scene-Level Dramatic Execution

### 12. Scene purpose

**Question:** What changes because this scene exists?

Every scene should materially alter knowledge, power, relationship, objective, danger, or commitment.

**Failure signs:** atmospheric repetition; redundant explanation; characters leaving with the same options and attitudes they entered with.

### 13. Beat progression

**Question:** Does the scene develop through distinct tactical beats?

**Look for:** action, resistance, adjustment, reversal, decision. Each beat should answer the previous one and create the next.

**Failure signs:** conversational circling; emotional intensity without tactical change; sudden scene conclusion.

### Supplemental diagnostic: Dialectical progression

**Question:** Does each dramatic unit establish a force, encounter meaningful counterforce, and produce a changed state?

**Core pattern:** **assertion → counterforce → changed state**

Apply this recursively at the level of the dialogue exchange, beat, scene, sequence, act, and whole story. Conflict alone is not enough. The opposition must alter the situation.

Do not require every exchange to complete the pattern independently. Judge whether the relevant dramatic unit produces meaningful alteration, and use the smallest unit that can fairly sustain the full pattern.

**Look for:** one action provokes a meaningful response; opposition forces adaptation; leverage shifts; strategy changes; the final state could not exist without the collision.

**Failure signs:** assertion followed by repetition rather than counterforce; opposition that does not alter strategy; arguments where neither side gains or loses leverage; scenes that end in essentially the same state in which they began; conflict that increases emotional volume without creating a new dramatic reality.

### 14. Subtext

**Question:** Is the immediate language carrying a second dramatic transaction?

**Look for:** concealment; testing; bargaining; status negotiation; emotional protection.

**Failure signs:** characters saying exactly what they mean at all times; exposition mistaken for dialogue.

### 15. Entry and exit

**Question:** Does the scene begin near the point of pressure and end on altered momentum?

**Failure signs:** throat-clearing; arrival logistics without consequence; ending after everyone has summarized what happened.

## Part IV: Reader Experience

### 16. Orientation and controlled uncertainty

**Question:** Does the audience understand enough to care while still needing to know more?

Mystery withholds answers. Confusion withholds the question itself.

**Look for:** clear immediate situation; purposeful unanswered questions; stable physical and causal grounding.

**Failure signs:** undefined terminology clusters; hidden basic staging; ambiguity used to cover missing logic.

### 17. Tension

**Question:** Is the audience anticipating a consequential outcome?

Tension requires desire, uncertainty, and consequence. Danger alone is insufficient.

**Look for:** delayed answers; vulnerable plans; conflicting hopes; consequences approaching before characters recognize them.

### 18. Surprise and inevitability

**Question:** Does the story create developments that are unexpected beforehand but coherent afterward?

**Failure signs:** random twists; obvious reveals delayed past credibility; retroactive rules introduced solely to enable an ending.

### 19. Emotional consequence

**Question:** Does the story make events matter through specific human—or character—experience?

**Look for:** emotional change expressed through action, perception, avoidance, or cost; aftermath that changes behavior.

**Failure signs:** emotion labels replacing dramatization; tragedy without attachment; immediate recovery after major events.

### 20. Momentum

**Question:** Does each passage create pressure to continue?

Momentum may come from danger, curiosity, emotional need, irony, dread, or desire—not only speed.

**Failure signs:** repeated information; scenes solving their own questions without opening stronger ones; explanations placed before the reader needs them.

## Part V: Meaning and Distinction

### 21. Thematic argument

**Question:** What competing truths does the story dramatize?

Theme is not the subject. “Power” is a subject. “Power survives by convincing its victims that obedience is safety” is an argument.

**Look for:** competing credible positions; theme embodied in decisions and consequences; ending that complicates or resolves the argument.

**Failure signs:** moral announced by dialogue; every sympathetic character sharing one position; symbols unconnected to action.

### 22. Image system and motifs

**Question:** Do recurring images accumulate or transform meaning?

**Look for:** repetition with variation; objects that carry memory or consequence; imagery linked to theme and character.

**Failure signs:** decorative symbols; motifs explained immediately; repeated images that never change function.

### 23. Voice

**Question:** Does the prose possess a consistent, precise way of seeing?

**Look for:** selective detail; rhythmic control; diction appropriate to viewpoint and world; sentences shaped by dramatic pressure.

**Failure signs:** generic cinematic description; ornamental language detached from perception; tonal drift.

### 24. Originality of combination

**Question:** Does the story combine familiar elements into a dramatic experience with its own identity?

Originality need not mean unprecedented ingredients. It means the story’s governing combination, perspective, or mechanism feels necessary rather than imitative.

**Failure signs:** recognizable premise with no transformed use; references doing the work of invention.

### 25. Economy and necessity

**Question:** Is each major element doing more than one job?

Strong elements often advance plot, reveal character, intensify theme, and shape atmosphere simultaneously.

**Failure signs:** lore with no dramatic function; side characters with one mechanical purpose; repeated proof of an already established fact.

## Part VI: Interactive-Fiction Rubric

Score this section only when the audience acts through choices, exploration, simulation, or systems.

### 26. Meaningful agency

**Question:** Do choices express intention and produce interpretable consequences?

Meaningful does not require every choice to create a separate plot. It requires the game to recognize what the player attempted.

**Failure signs:** cosmetic wording leading to identical response; unknowable choices; consequences unrelated to the selected intention.

### 27. Choice legibility

**Question:** Does the player understand the attitude, tactic, or risk represented by each option?

The outcome may surprise the player, but the intended action should not.

**Failure signs:** dialogue option says one thing and character performs another; hidden moral scoring; choices distinguished only by tone.

### 28. Consequence depth

**Question:** How deeply does the work remember a choice?

Evaluate consequences at four levels:

1. Immediate response
2. Later callback
3. Changed available strategy
4. Changed interpretation or ending

A strong interactive story uses all four selectively.

### 29. Branch differentiation

**Question:** Do different approaches generate different dramatic experiences?

Branches may reconverge, but they should arrive with different knowledge, relationships, costs, strategies, or emotional meanings. Distinguish cosmetic variation, informational variation, tactical variation, causal variation, and interpretive variation.

**Failure signs:** every route reveals the same exposition; choices only reorder paragraphs; convergence erases prior action.

### 30. Reconvergence quality

**Question:** When branches meet again, does the story preserve what made them different?

Good reconvergence carries state forward through altered text, available actions, power, knowledge, or interpretation.

### 31. State as storytelling

**Question:** Do variables, inventory, memory, repetition, or system rules communicate story rather than merely track progress?

**Look for:** mechanics that embody theme; remembered details; altered interface; state-dependent meaning.

### 32. Failure quality

**Question:** Are failure, death, looping, and blocked choices dramatically productive?

**Look for:** new knowledge; changed context; meaningful cost; a failure that becomes part of the character’s history.

**Failure signs:** reload as erasure; punishment without insight; arbitrary lockouts.

### 33. Player–character relationship

**Question:** Is the distance between player knowledge, character knowledge, and available action deliberate?

**Look for:** productive dramatic irony; choices suited to the established character; conscious use of resistance between player and protagonist.

### 34. Interface–theme unity

**Question:** Does the act of interacting participate in the story’s meaning?

This is the highest interactive standard. The audience should lose something if the story were converted unchanged into prose or film.

### 35. Ending recognition

**Question:** Do endings reflect the player’s accumulated pattern of action rather than only the final selection?

**Failure signs:** ending menu disguised as climax; one late choice overriding the entire playthrough; “true ending” unlocked by arbitrary collection.

## Part VII: Prose and Presentation Control

### 36. Precision

Do verbs, nouns, spatial details, and sensory information create exact action and perception?

### 37. Rhythm

Does sentence and paragraph rhythm respond to pressure, reflection, revelation, and impact?

### 38. Exposition discipline

Is information delivered when it becomes dramatically necessary, through conflict or discovery whenever possible?

### 39. Dialogue integrity

Does each speaker pursue something? Are voices distinguishable through thought and tactic—not gimmick?

### 40. Technical polish

Check grammar, spelling, punctuation, formatting, continuity, broken links, repeated text, variable errors, and interface consistency.

Technical polish cannot rescue weak drama, but technical errors can destroy immersion and trust.

## Diagnostic Priority

Do not convert section priority into a single final quality score.

For a conventional linear story:

| Section | Priority |
|---|---|
| Architecture | Load-bearing |
| Character and relationships | Load-bearing |
| Scene execution | Major |
| Reader experience | Major |
| Meaning and distinction | Major |
| Prose and presentation | Supporting until architecture is sound; decisive at publication stage |

For interactive fiction:

| Section | Priority |
|---|---|
| Architecture | Load-bearing |
| Character and relationships | Load-bearing |
| Interactivity | Load-bearing |
| Scene execution | Major |
| Reader experience | Major |
| Meaning and distinction | Major |
| Prose and presentation | Supporting until architecture is sound; decisive at publication stage |

## Catastrophic-Failure Gates

Regardless of total score, flag a work as **not ready** if any of these applies:

- The protagonist has no intelligible objective or pressure.
- Major events lack causal connection.
- The protagonist has no meaningful causal ownership over decisive turns.
- The consequences do not become more significant, personal, specific, or irreversible in a manner appropriate to the work.
- Major dramatic units repeatedly produce no changed state.
- The climax does not arise from the story’s central conflict.
- Essential world or action logic is incomprehensible.
- Interactive choices repeatedly misrepresent the resulting action.
- Branches erase player intention without recognition.
- The ending depends on a rule introduced only at the ending.
- Technical failures prevent completion.

## Evaluation Procedure

### Pass 1: Experience

Read or play without stopping to edit. Record only:

- What you wanted to happen
- What you feared would happen
- Where attention intensified
- Where attention weakened
- What confused you
- What remained afterward

### Pass 2: Structural map

Identify:

- Dramatic proposition
- Central objective
- Principal opposition
- Major decisions
- Stakes progression
- Causal ownership of decisive turns
- Turning points
- Irreversible changes
- Climax
- Resolution
- Thematic argument

For interactive work, map state changes, branch points, reconvergences, loops, locks, endings, persistent consequences, and whether the system recognizes player intention.

### Pass 3: Score with evidence

Score each applicable category. Every score must cite at least one concrete passage, scene, choice, or system behavior. A number without evidence is not an evaluation. Do not produce an aggregate quality score.

### Pass 4: Diagnose causes

Do not report only symptoms such as “slow,” “confusing,” or “flat.” Name the dramatic cause.

Examples:

- **Symptom:** The middle drags.  
  **Cause:** Three successive scenes test the same loyalty without changing leverage or consequence.

- **Symptom:** The twist feels random.  
  **Cause:** The revelation changes the explanation of past events but does not emerge from previously visible evidence.

- **Symptom:** Choices feel fake.  
  **Cause:** Aggression, curiosity, and obedience all produce the same response and grant identical knowledge.

### Pass 5: Prioritize revisions

Classify recommendations:

- **Load-bearing:** must change or the story fails
- **High leverage:** one change improves several categories
- **Local:** scene, beat, prose, or interface repair
- **Optional:** taste-based enhancement

Never begin with line editing when the dramatic architecture is broken.

## Final Evaluation Template

### Governing judgment

In one paragraph, state what the story is attempting, what genuinely works, and what currently prevents it from reaching its strongest form.

### Strongest element

Name the single element that should be protected through revision. Explain why it generates meaning.

### Load-bearing weakness

Name the one weakness whose repair would most improve the work. Identify its cause, not merely its symptom.

### Stakes progression

Describe how the consequences of success, failure, delay, or refusal change across the story. Identify whether the stakes become greater, more personal, more specific, and more irreversible.

### Causal ownership

Identify who causes the major turns. Flag decisive changes primarily caused by coincidence, rescue, antagonist stupidity, system fiat, or authorial convenience.

### Dialectical progression

Identify the strongest and weakest examples of **assertion → counterforce → changed state**. Evaluate this at the scene level and, where useful, at the act or whole-story level.

### Category scores

List applicable categories with scores and specific evidence. Do not calculate a single aggregate score.

### Reader/player experience

Describe the experience chronologically: engagement, expectation, uncertainty, pressure, payoff, and residue.

### Revision priorities

1. Load-bearing repair
2. Highest-leverage dramatic improvement
3. Character or relationship deepening
4. Scene/beat restructuring
5. Prose and technical polish

### Verdict

Choose one:

- **Concept exploration** — the governing idea exists, but the drama is not yet built.
- **Functional draft** — the story works but remains generic, uneven, or underdeveloped.
- **Strong draft** — the major drama works; targeted revision can make it publishable.
- **Publishable/professional** — coherent, controlled, emotionally effective, and technically sound.
- **Distinctive achievement** — the form, drama, and meaning reinforce one another in a way that feels difficult to replace.

## Governing Principle

The rubric exists to sharpen judgment, not automate taste. A story is not the sum of forty scores. The final question is:

> **What experience did the story create, how did it create it, and was that experience worth the audience’s attention?**
